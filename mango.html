Mango

1. description

Mango is one of the most popular tropical fruit trees in the world. It belongs to the genus Mangifera and the family Anacardiaceae. Its scientific name is Mangifera indica. Mango is widely known for its delicious, sweet and juicy fruits, which are enjoyed in many countries.

Mango is a large, evergreen and long-lived tree that can grow up to 30–40 metres tall under favourable natural conditions. It has a strong trunk, spreading branches and a dense, rounded canopy. Its leaves are simple, elongated and leathery, with a glossy surface. Young leaves often appear reddish, bronze or purplish before turning dark green as they mature.

Mango trees produce small, fragrant flowers arranged in large, branched clusters called panicles. The flowers are usually yellowish-white or pale green. Both male and bisexual flowers may occur in the same flower cluster. Following successful pollination, some flowers develop into fruits.

The mango fruit is botanically classified as a drupe. It generally has a thin skin, fleshy edible pulp and a large, hard seed in the centre. Depending on the variety, mature fruits may be green, yellow, orange or reddish. The pulp varies in colour, texture, sweetness and fibre content.

Mango trees are native to South Asia and have been cultivated for thousands of years. They are now grown throughout tropical and subtropical regions of the world. Due to their valuable fruits, attractive evergreen foliage and economic importance, mango trees are among the most widely cultivated fruit trees in tropical countries.

2. Habitat and distribution:

Native distribution: Mango (Mangifera indica) is native to South Asia, particularly the region covering present-day northeastern India, Bangladesh and Myanmar. It has been cultivated in this region for thousands of years and gradually spread to other tropical and subtropical parts of the world.

Natural habitat: Wild mango trees are commonly associated with tropical forests, particularly tropical moist and dry forests. They can also grow in woodland areas, forest margins and open landscapes. Mango trees generally thrive in warm environments with adequate sunlight and suitable soil conditions.

Climate: Mango trees are well adapted to tropical and subtropical climates. They grow best in warm conditions with a distinct dry period that encourages flowering. Mature trees can tolerate short periods of drought, although young trees require adequate moisture for healthy development.

Soil and geographical conditions: Mango trees grow in a variety of soil types, including sandy, loamy and clayey soils, provided that drainage is good. They are commonly found in lowland areas, plains and gently sloping landscapes. Deep soils allow their root systems to develop properly.

Global distribution: Mango trees are now cultivated throughout tropical and subtropical regions of the world. Major mango-growing regions include South Asia, Southeast Asia, Africa, Central America, South America and parts of Australia. India, China, Indonesia, Pakistan, Mexico and Thailand are among the important mango-producing countries.

Distribution in Bangladesh: Mango trees are widely distributed throughout Bangladesh. Important mango-growing districts include Rajshahi, Chapainawabganj, Naogaon, Dinajpur, Rangpur, Satkhira and Meherpur. Mango orchards, homestead gardens and agricultural fields are common places where mango trees are found.

3. Uses and importance

Food and nutrition: Mango is an important tropical fruit widely consumed around the world. Its sweet, juicy pulp is rich in vitamins, particularly vitamin C and vitamin A precursors (carotenoids). It also contains dietary fibre, antioxidants and several essential minerals.

Fresh fruit consumption: Ripe mangoes are commonly eaten fresh as a dessert or snack. They are also used in fruit salads, smoothies, juices, milkshakes and various sweet dishes.

Food processing: Mangoes are widely used in the food-processing industry to produce juice, jam, jelly, chutney, pickles, squash, dried fruit and canned products. Both ripe and unripe mangoes have important culinary uses.

Traditional culinary importance: Unripe green mangoes are commonly used in pickles, chutneys, sauces and traditional dishes. In Bangladesh and other South Asian countries, raw mango is particularly popular in seasonal foods and refreshments.

Economic importance: Mango is one of the most commercially valuable fruit crops in tropical countries. Mango cultivation provides income and employment opportunities for farmers, orchard workers, traders, transporters and people involved in fruit processing and marketing.

Medicinal and nutritional importance: Mango fruits contain antioxidants and nutrients that contribute to a balanced diet. Mango leaves, bark and seeds have also been used in traditional medicine in different cultures. However, traditional medicinal uses should not be considered proven treatments for diseases.

Industrial uses: Mango kernels contain starch and fat that have potential applications in food and cosmetic industries. Mango seed kernel extracts and other fruit-processing by-products are also being explored for industrial and commercial applications.

Environmental importance: Mango trees provide shade, help support local biodiversity and offer food and shelter for birds, insects and other organisms. Their extensive root systems can also contribute to soil stability.

Cultural importance: Mango has significant cultural importance in South Asia. In Bangladesh, it is often called the King of Fruits and is closely associated with summer, seasonal traditions, festivals and hospitality.

4. Cultivation:

Climate and temperature: Mango trees grow best in tropical and subtropical climates. They prefer warm weather, plenty of sunlight and a distinct dry period before flowering. Mature trees can tolerate moderately dry conditions, but young plants are more sensitive to drought and cold temperatures.

Sunlight requirements: Mango trees require full sunlight for healthy growth and fruit production. A location receiving approximately 6–8 hours or more of direct sunlight daily is suitable. Adequate sunlight supports flowering, fruit development and the growth of a healthy canopy.

Soil requirements: Mango trees grow well in deep, fertile and well-drained soil. Loamy and sandy loam soils are particularly suitable. The soil should contain sufficient organic matter and allow roots to spread deeply. Waterlogged soil should be avoided because it can cause root damage.

Propagation: Mango trees can be propagated through seeds and vegetative methods. However, grafting is commonly preferred for commercial cultivation because it helps maintain the characteristics of selected varieties and allows trees to produce fruits earlier than seed-grown trees.

Planting: Healthy grafted mango seedlings should be selected for planting. Planting is generally carried out at the beginning of the rainy season when sufficient soil moisture is available. A suitable planting pit should be prepared and filled with topsoil mixed with well-decomposed organic manure. Adequate spacing should be maintained between trees to allow proper sunlight and air circulation.

Watering: Newly planted mango trees require regular watering to establish their root systems. Young trees should receive sufficient moisture, particularly during dry periods. Mature trees are relatively drought-tolerant, but supplementary irrigation during fruit development can improve fruit growth and quality. Excessive watering should be avoided.

Fertilization: Mango trees benefit from regular applications of organic manure and balanced fertilizers. Nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium are important nutrients for healthy growth, flowering and fruit production. Fertilizer requirements increase as the tree grows and begins bearing fruits. Application rates should be adjusted according to tree age, soil fertility and local agricultural recommendations.

Weed management: Weeds around mango trees compete for water, sunlight and nutrients. Regular removal of weeds helps young trees establish properly. Organic mulching around the base can help conserve soil moisture and suppress weed growth. Mulch should not be placed directly against the trunk.

Pruning and training: Pruning helps develop a strong and well-shaped tree structure. During the early growth stages, unwanted shoots and weak branches can be removed to encourage a balanced canopy. Dead, damaged, diseased and overcrowded branches should also be removed to improve air circulation and sunlight penetration.

Pest and disease management: Mango trees may be affected by pests such as mango hoppers, fruit flies, mealybugs and scale insects. Common diseases include anthracnose, powdery mildew and bacterial black spot. Regular monitoring, orchard sanitation, proper pruning and appropriate pest management practices help protect the trees.

Flowering and fruit development: Mango trees generally flower during a relatively dry period. Their small flowers develop in branched clusters called panicles. After successful pollination and fertilization, some flowers develop into young fruits. Fruit development usually takes several months, depending on the variety and environmental conditions.

Fruit thinning and protection: When a tree produces an excessive number of young fruits, thinning may help reduce competition and improve the size and quality of the remaining fruits. Fruit bagging can also protect developing mangoes from certain insect pests and physical damage.

Harvesting: Mango fruits are harvested when they reach physiological maturity. Signs of maturity may include changes in fruit shape, size, skin colour and firmness, depending on the variety. Fruits should be handled carefully during harvesting to prevent bruising and damage.

Container cultivation: Dwarf and compact mango varieties can be grown in large containers. A deep pot with drainage holes and a fertile, well-drained growing medium is necessary. Container-grown mango trees require sufficient sunlight, regular watering, suitable fertilization and occasional pruning.
